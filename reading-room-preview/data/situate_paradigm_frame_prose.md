## The paradigms they think with

A **Paradigm**, in the instrument's vocabulary, is a *cultural script a text reaches for* — a ready-made
pattern of roles and expected outcomes, tagged with the stance the text takes toward it (does it
**endorse** the script, merely **invoke** it, quietly **subvert** it, or flatly **deny** it?). Coding the
four resurrection chapters this way turns up one striking agreement and four distinct signatures.

The agreement first. All four gospels reach, above every other script, for the same one — and all four
**endorse** it at the very top of the scale. The instrument's name for it is the **vindication of the
righteous sufferer**: the deep expectation, alive in the Judaism of the time, that God will publicly
overturn the verdict passed on a faithful person who was unjustly destroyed. You can hear the script in a
single fused line the coding flags in three of the four — "Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has
been raised" — where the shameful execution and the divine reversal are bound in one breath. The empty
tomb, in all four, is legible as *vindication*, not as mere disappearance. That is the shared floor. What
follows is what each gospel builds on it.

**Matthew — the vindication staged as theophany and reversal.** On top of the shared script, Matthew
piles two more it endorses almost as strongly: **angelic commissioning** (the messenger's fixed sequence —
"Do not be afraid," the announcement, "Come, see the place," "go quickly and tell") and a full
**theophany**, a Sinai-style divine descent (the earthquake, the angel "descending from heaven," the face
"like lightning," clothing "white as snow"). And it adds a reversal the others only imply: the coded
**honor–shame** and **holy-war** scripts, in which the armed guards — the muscle of the executing power —
are struck down "like dead men" without a blow, while the crucified one is raised and the low-status women
are elevated into heralds. Matthew even sets up the ordinary script of a mourning-visit to a grave and
**subverts** it: the women come "to see the tomb," and the visit breaks open into a commissioning. The
distinctive Matthean move is to make the vindication *visible* — dramatized as a small Sinai and a public
overturning of honor.

**Mark — the vindication that ends in failed speech.** Mark endorses the same dominant script, then
frames the scene with a second the others don't foreground: the **translation-of-the-hero** template (a
missing body plus a heavenly interpreter, read as removal-to-God rather than corpse-theft). But Mark's
true signature is a *stance*, not a script. It takes the recurring pattern of the **failed disciple** — the
witness who cannot speak — and **ironizes** it: the women "went out and fled… they said nothing to anyone,
for they were afraid." The proclamation is set up and then withheld, thrown onto the reader. Mark also sets
up the burial-anointing rite (the women buy spices to anoint the body) only to **subvert** it — the corpse
is gone, so the rite cannot complete. Where Matthew dramatizes, Mark unsettles: the vindication is
announced, and the human response is terror and silence.

**Luke — the vindication read by remembering.** Luke's second engine, endorsed as strongly as the first,
is **prophecy-fulfillment as remembering**. The empty tomb here is not self-interpreting; it becomes
resurrection only when routed back through Jesus's own earlier word — "Remember how he told you, while he
was still in Galilee…" and then "they remembered his words." The interpretive turn is *recall*, not sight.
Luke also does something pointed with a legal script: it sets up the women as a formal witness-list, lets
the eleven **dismiss** their report as "an idle tale" (the technical word for unreliable testimony), and
then **subverts** that dismissal — the narrator names the witnesses and lets Peter's run tacitly ratify
them. And Luke alone flatly **denies** a script the others leave available: not "he has been taken"
(assumption, translation) but "he is not here, but has risen." The distinctive Lukan move is to make
memory the key that turns an empty tomb into a resurrection — and to vindicate the women the culture would
discount.

**John — the vindication seen and believed.** John endorses the shared script and pairs it with a
**recognition-scene** (*anagnorisis*): discipleship rendered as a graded act of *seeing* — the race to the
tomb, the stooping look, the entering, and finally "he saw and believed." Its sharpest coded stance is a
**subversion** of the **grave-robbery** script: Mary's twice-stated hypothesis, "they have taken the Lord
out of the tomb," sets up a mundane theft — and the neatly folded head-cloth, "rolled up in a place by
itself," is the anti-clue that undoes it (thieves do not unwrap a body and fold the cloth aside). John also
runs a quiet **authority contest** between the beloved disciple and Peter — the one whom Jesus loved
outruns Peter, defers at the entrance, then believes first — ranking insight-through-love above institutional
precedence. John's distinctive move is to make the empty tomb *evidence*: something looked at, inferred
from, and believed.

## Four voices, four lenses

Below the paradigms, the instrument records a **Frame** for each gospel — its position on a fixed set of
axes that ask, in effect, *how does this text know what it claims, and what kind of world does it assume?*
Two of those axes carry the cleanest contrast among the four, and they are what the radar viz is built to
show.

**The first axis is the epistemic warrant — the grounds on which each gospel rests its claim.** Two values
recur across all four: **revelation** (the truth is *disclosed*, announced by a heavenly messenger) and
**testimony** (the truth is *reported*, carried by a witness who was there). Every gospel uses both. What
divides them is which one leads.

- **Matthew and Mark lead with revelation.** In both, the resurrection is grounded first in angelic
  disclosure — the messenger simply *tells* the women what has happened before anyone weighs a single
  clue. (Matthew ranks revelation highest, at high confidence; testimony follows, once the news is handed
  on: "go quickly and tell.")
- **Luke and John lead with testimony.** In both, the top warrant is eyewitness report. Luke's women
  "told all this to the eleven and to all the rest"; John's whole tomb scene is a careful chain of *who
  saw what, in what order* — Mary sees the stone gone, the beloved disciple sees the wrappings, Peter
  enters and sees, the beloved disciple enters and believes. Revelation is present in both, but ranked
  second.

So the four split evenly on the question *how do you know the tomb is empty?* — two answer "it was
announced," two answer "it was witnessed." That even split is the spine of the radar.

**The second axis is the ontological commitment — what kind of reality each gospel is willing to rest
weight on.** Here three of the four agree and one adds something. All four are coded **transcendent-personal**
at the top: the reality asserted is a personal, transcendent God who acts (an angel descends; "he has been
raised" in the divine passive, the raiser unspoken but understood). **John, and John alone, adds a second
commitment the others lack: the material.** It lingers on the physical remains — the linen wrappings lying
where the body had been, and the head-cloth "not lying with the other linen cloths, but rolled up in a
place by itself." For John, the material world *matters and bears the evidence*; the empty tomb is not only
announced and witnessed but *inspected*. That extra axis is what makes John's shape on the radar bulge
where the others are flat.

There is a quieter third contrast worth a line, because the coding carries it: **how each gospel reasons
from the empty tomb.** Matthew reasons by **cause** — the earthquake happens "*for* an angel of the Lord…
came and rolled back the stone." Luke reasons by **exegesis** — the tomb is read by *recalling* prior
prophecy ("Remember how he told you…"). Mark and John both reason by **abduction**, inference to the best
explanation — an empty tomb plus an interpreting sign yields "he has been raised" (Mark), and the orderly
grave-cloths yield "he saw and believed" (John). Four gospels, four ways of getting from an empty space to
a claim: by cause, by remembered text, and — twice — by weighing the evidence.
