## The shared world

Before the four gospels differ — and they differ more than most readers expect — they agree on the
world they are written into. Not the world they describe, which is Galilee and Jerusalem around the
year 30. The world they are written *from*, a half-century later.

That later world had just been broken. Between 66 and 73 the province of Judea rose against Rome in
what we now call the **First Jewish–Roman War** (the failed revolt that ended in Roman reconquest). Its
hinge event came in the year 70: Roman legions took Jerusalem and destroyed the **Second Temple** — the
single building that had organized Jewish worship, pilgrimage, and sacrifice for centuries. It did not
just fall. It stopped being available. The place where heaven and earth were understood to meet was
rubble, and it stayed rubble.

Every reader of these four accounts should hold one fact steady: all four resurrection chapters were
composed *after* that. The instrument codes each of them into the same situational conditions — its
term is **Sitz** (the situational conditions of a text: when it was written, where, and what the world
was doing to the people who wrote it) — and the shared reading is blunt. When the Temple is gone, a
tradition has to find somewhere else to stand. Authority has to move. It cannot live in a building that
no longer exists.

This is what the empty tomb is doing in all four gospels at once. It relocates the center. The living
weight of the tradition shifts off the destroyed Temple and onto the risen Christ — a person the
movement claims is present, findable, and speaking, precisely when the old sacred place is not.
"He is not here," the messenger says in three of the four, and the sentence carries more than one
absence: not here in the tomb, and — for the communities reading it after 70 — the holy is *not there*
either, not in the ash where the Temple stood. It is somewhere new.

The instrument gives all four the same one-word label for this moment in a tradition's life:
**Formation** (the coded phase in which a tradition is still building the forms it will later take for
granted). These are not settled scriptures being quoted by a settled church. They are a movement under
pressure, working out where it lives now.

They were also written under Rome, and they know it. The crucifixion happens under Roman judicial power;
a Roman guard is posted at the tomb; the whole scene assumes the ordinary machinery of empire — roads,
courts, soldiers, the long-distance reach of a Mediterranean world that the apostle Paul's missionary
network had already been seeding for a generation. That reach is why a story told in Aramaic-speaking
Galilee could travel, in Greek, to house-churches scattered across the empire and be read there as news.

Rome is not only the setting; it is part of the argument. The very word the gospels use for their
message — *euangelion*, "good news" — was already imperial vocabulary, used for the announcements of an
emperor's birth or victory. Crucifixion was Rome's public instrument of terror, reserved for rebels and
slaves. So to say that a crucified man is Lord, and that *his* rising is the good news, is to borrow the
empire's own words and turn them. Scholars are careful here: the political charge was *available* in that
world, not stamped on every sentence. But it was available, and the gospels use it.

**A myth worth retiring.** You may have heard that a "Council of Jamnia" met around the year 90 and, in a
single sitting, closed the Jewish canon and expelled Christians from the synagogue. It is a tidy story,
and it is almost certainly not what happened. Most scholars now treat the "Council of Jamnia" as a modern
construction. What actually took place at Yavneh was slower and messier — a decades-long regathering of
the Pharisaic and scribal movement after 70, out of which rabbinic Judaism gradually grew. There was a
liturgical curse on "heretics" (the *birkat ha-minim*), and it may have been felt locally by some
Jewish followers of Jesus. But Reuven Kimelman and Daniel Boyarin have shown it was not an empire-wide
edict flushing Christians out of every synagogue at a stroke. The lesson matters for reading these
gospels: **the parting of Judaism and Christianity was gradual, regional, and untidy** — not a clean
early divorce, and certainly not a single day's decree.

That untidiness is why one feature of these texts must be read with care. Matthew's fierce rebukes of
"the scribes and Pharisees," and John's repeated "the Jews" (in Greek, *hoi Ioudaioi*), are the language
of an *intra-Jewish* argument — Jews contesting other Jews over who speaks for Israel after the
catastrophe. E. P. Sanders and N. T. Wright both insist on the point: these are Jewish texts, engaged in
Jewish debates. Centuries later that same language was lifted out of its family quarrel and turned into
generalized anti-Judaism, with real and terrible consequences. A reader today holds both facts at once:
what the words were doing in the first century, and what they were later made to do.

One crisis, then — but four different pressures inside it. The corpus and the scholarship agree on the
shape of the four inflections, and it is the cleanest way into the individual gospels:

- **Mark** is the gospel of the emergency — written closest to the war, all apocalyptic urgency and a
  suffering Messiah for a suffering church.
- **Matthew** is the family quarrel — Jesus as the authoritative interpreter of the Torah he fulfills
  rather than abolishes, a community arguing that *it* is true Israel.
- **Luke** (with its second volume, Acts) is the long apologetic history — the movement's story carried
  from Jerusalem all the way to Rome, and repeatedly found politically innocent along the way.
- **John** is the severed branch — a community that has felt the synagogue door close behind it, and
  answers with the highest claims about Jesus of any gospel.

The rest of this page follows those four pressures through three questions: *when* each gospel was
written, *where* and for whom, and *why* anyone wrote it down at all.

## When were they written?

Start with the one thing nearly every scholar agrees on: **Mark came first.** This is the near-universal
consensus — Markan priority (the position that Mark is the earliest gospel, and that Matthew and Luke
each had a copy of Mark open in front of them as they wrote). It is the bedrock of the whole chronology,
and once you have it, the rest of the ordering falls into place: two later gospels build on an earlier
one.

The fixed point in time is the year 70. The destruction of the Temple is the event the dating hangs on,
because the gospels seem to *know* it has happened. Watch two sentences. In Matthew, a king in a parable
sends troops who "destroyed those murderers, and burned their city" — a line most scholars read as
written *after* the fact, with the smoke of 70 already in view; Davies and Allison, in the standard
critical commentary on Matthew, read it exactly this way. In Luke, Jesus foretells a Jerusalem
"surrounded by armies," a description that matches the Roman siege closely enough that scholars take it,
too, as written looking back. The technical name for this is *ex eventu* prophecy — a prediction composed
after the event it "predicts." It is one of the clearest tools we have for dating an ancient text: a
gospel that describes the fall of Jerusalem in detail was very probably written after Jerusalem fell.

From there the coded spine and the scholarship line up cleanly. The instrument anchors the four at
**Mark ~70, Matthew ~80, Luke ~85, John ~95** — and every one of those anchors sits comfortably inside
the ranges the scholarship gives:

- **Mark, around 70.** Written in the immediate shadow of the war. Scholars debate only which side of 70
  it sits on — Adela Yarbro Collins leans slightly *before*, since Mark's prophecy doesn't quite describe
  the Temple *burning*; Joel Marcus and others read it *during or just after*. The disagreement is a
  matter of a few years around a single hinge.
- **Matthew, the 80s.** Long enough after Mark for Matthew to rework it thoroughly, and clearly aware of
  the Temple's fall.
- **Luke, the 80s to 90s** — with one genuinely open argument, below.
- **John, around 90–110.** The latest, and not written all at once: the scholarship (Raymond Brown's
  work above all) describes John as composed in *stages* over decades, which is why a single tidy date
  never quite fits it.

Two honest edges to name:

**Luke has a live minority argument.** The mainstream places Luke–Acts in the 80s–90s. But a serious
group of scholars — Richard Pervo and Joseph Tyson foremost — argues for a later date, into the early
second century (around 100–120), partly on the grounds that Luke seems to know the historian Josephus,
who wrote in the 90s. This is not a fringe view; it is a real and growing position. Where the field
lands: mainstream 80s–90s, with a serious later-dating minority.

**The early-dating position is a fringe.** At the other end, a small camp (associated with John A. T.
Robinson) argues all the gospels were written *before* 70. It exists, and it should be named as existing
— but it sits well outside the consensus.

## Where, and for whom

Here honesty comes first: **we do not know exactly where any of the four gospels was written.** None of
them says. Scholars triangulate — from the language a gospel uses, the customs it stops to explain, the
community tensions it reveals, and the earliest outside witnesses who quote it. What follows is
probability, not address.

**Mark — traditionally Rome; perhaps the Roman East.** The oldest tradition places Mark in Rome, and the
internal clues fit: Mark is full of Latin loanwords (military and administrative terms a Roman audience
would know), it translates every Aramaic phrase and explains Jewish customs — the marks of a writer whose
readers are largely *Gentile* (non-Jewish) and unfamiliar with Jewish practice. That said, a serious
modern alternative places Mark in Syria or the wider Roman East, closer to the war. The careful
formulation, which the research engines themselves split on, is: **traditionally Rome, with Syria a real
alternative** — not a settled question.

**Matthew — probably Syria, perhaps Antioch.** Matthew's community looks like a mixed Jewish–Gentile
congregation in a large Hellenized city, locked in an intense argument with the emerging rabbinic
movement over who rightly interprets the Torah — notice how often it says "*their* synagogues," the
language of a group defining itself against a body it once belonged to. Antioch in Syria is the usual
best guess, supported by the fact that Ignatius, a bishop of Antioch writing around 110, is among the
earliest to echo Matthew. Best read as "probably Syria, perhaps Antioch."

**Luke — the hardest to place; an unknown Greek-speaking city somewhere in the eastern Mediterranean.**
Luke is the most polished Greek of the four and the least willing to give itself away. It is addressed to
a "Theophilus" — either a real patron or, since the name means "lover of God," an emblem for the ideal
reader — and it is pitched to a largely Gentile audience, forever locating the story inside *world*
history (emperors, censuses, governors) rather than only Jewish history. Its home city is genuinely
unknown.

**John — traditionally Ephesus; possibly Syria.** John reads like the voice of a distinct, wounded
community — one that has been *put out* of the synagogue, and whose language turns sharply dualistic
(light against darkness, from above against from below) as a result. J. Louis Martyn's classic study
read the whole gospel as a two-level drama: Jesus' story told so that it doubles as the story of John's
own expelled community. Tradition (from Irenaeus) places it at Ephesus in Asia Minor; some scholars
argue for Syria. Either way, the *situation* — a group that has lost its synagogue home — is clearer than
the geography.

One caution worth carrying. For a generation, scholars spoke confidently of each gospel's
own "community," as if written for one local congregation. Richard Bauckham pushed back hard, arguing the
gospels were written for wide circulation — "for all Christians," not one town. The community model is
still the working majority, but Bauckham's challenge is the reason to hold it loosely: when we say "the
Johannine community," we are reconstructing, not reporting.

## Why these four wrote it down

Two puzzles sit under this question. Who wrote these books — and why did anyone commit them to writing
when they did?

**Start with a fact that surprises many readers: all four gospels are anonymous.** Not one names its
author inside the text. The familiar titles — "according to Matthew," "according to Mark" — were attached
later, in the late first or early second century, to secure the books to apostolic authority. Bart Ehrman
is the scholar most associated with putting this plainly: the titles are labels added by the early church,
not signatures. The instrument's own coding agrees from the other direction — every coded move registers
the teller as "the evangelist, anonymous, writing in the third person," with authority resting on
*received tradition* rather than on a named eyewitness.

The earliest outside comment we have on any of this comes from **Papias**, a bishop writing around 110–125,
quoted much later by the historian Eusebius. Papias says Mark was the "interpreter" of Peter and wrote
accurately but "not in order," and that Matthew compiled the sayings "in the Hebrew dialect." Almost every
discussion of gospel authorship since is, in some sense, an argument with those two sentences. Here is
where the scholarship lands on each:

- **Mark** — an unknown Greek-speaking Christian, possibly named Mark. The ancient link to Peter is
  possible and unprovable; the gospel does not read like a seamless eyewitness memoir.
- **Matthew** — not the apostle, but an anonymous Jewish-Christian *scribe* — a trained interpreter of
  scripture. The decisive clue is literary: this gospel copies some 80–90% of Mark, and an eyewitness
  apostle would hardly have built his account on the framework of a non-eyewitness.
- **Luke** — the same author as Acts: a highly literate, non-eyewitness Greek writer, steeped in the
  Septuagint and in Greco-Roman history-writing. Whether he was, as tradition holds, a companion of Paul
  is genuinely contested. Joseph Fitzmyer's major commentary is the standard guide to the debate.
- **John** — the gospel credits its testimony to an unnamed "Beloved Disciple," and the late tradition
  identifying him as John son of Zebedee is doubted by most scholars. Raymond Brown's widely accepted
  reconstruction sees the book built up in stages: a founding eyewitness, an evangelist who wrote around
  90, and a later editor who added the final chapter after the Beloved Disciple had died.

None of this is skepticism for its own sake. It is why the *second* puzzle — why write it down, and why
then — has such a clear answer. For a generation, the Jesus movement had lived on spoken testimony;
eyewitnesses were still alive, and many expected the end of the age within their lifetimes. Around the
same window, four pressures converged:

1. **The eyewitnesses were dying.** Peter and Paul were gone (traditionally under Nero, ~64–67); the
   founding generation was passing, and with it the living memory.
2. **The catastrophe of 70** demanded interpretation. A destroyed Temple was not a footnote; it was a
   theological emergency that had to be answered in narrative.
3. **The end had not come.** As the expectation of an imminent return faded, the movement had to prepare
   for a long stretch of ordinary history — which meant durable, teachable texts.
4. **The communities were scattered and Greek-speaking.** Dispersed, increasingly Gentile congregations
   needed portable accounts they could use — for teaching new members, for worship, for settling disputes,
   for holding an identity together across great distances.

The form they reached for was one their world already knew: the *bios*, the Greco-Roman "life" — a
biography organized to reveal the character and significance of its subject. A
gospel is not a modern biography and not a neutral chronicle; it is a *life* written to be used.

## Matthew — a worked reading

*(carried over from v1 as the voice-proof for the individual-gospel pages; its two stubs are now closed by
the sections above — Matthew's dating reasoning is in "When were they written?", its geography and
authorship in "Where, and for whom" and "Why these four wrote it down.")*

Start with Matthew, and start with how it opens the morning.

Two women named Mary walk to a grave at first light. That is the whole of the human action for a moment
— an errand, a visit to the dead, the most ordinary grief in the world. And then the ground moves.
"Behold, there was a great earthquake, for an angel of the Lord descended from the sky, and came and
rolled away the stone from the door, and sat on it. His appearance was like lightning, and his clothing
white as snow." The guards posted to secure the tomb shake and go rigid — "became like dead men." The
women get a command, a fact, and an errand of their own: *don't be afraid; he is not here, for he has
risen; go quickly and tell.*

Notice what kind of scene this is. Matthew doesn't ease you toward the resurrection through a slow
discovery. It hands the news to you the way the women get it — announced, from the sky, before anyone
has looked inside anything. The instrument names the pattern the text is thinking with. Its term is
**Paradigm** (a cultural script a text reaches for, tagged with the text's stance toward it), and
Matthew's dominant script is coded at the very top of its salience: **the eschatological vindication of
the righteous sufferer** — the deep expectation, alive in the Judaism of the time, that God will
publicly overturn the verdict passed on a faithful person who was unjustly destroyed.

Hold those two phrases together, because the whole chapter turns on their collision. "Jesus, who has
been crucified" is the verdict of the powers — the shameful, official, Roman judgment that this man was
a criminal and is finished. "He has risen, just like he said" is the counter-verdict, and it comes from
heaven's own messenger. The empty tomb is where the second sentence overrules the first. And Matthew
stages the reversal physically: the armed guards, the muscle of the executing authority, are the ones
struck down "like dead men," while the executed one is the one raised. The powers that had all the honor
end the scene face-down in the dirt. The women — low-status witnesses in that world, the kind of
witnesses a Roman court would not have credited — are the ones handed the message to carry. That
inversion is not decoration. It is the argument.

This is also why Matthew leads the way it does. The instrument gives every text a **Frame** (its
coordinate on a fixed set of axes — in plain terms, *how the text knows what it claims*), and the
first thing Matthew's Frame records is its top epistemic warrant: **revelation**. The resurrection here
is not first inferred, not first argued, not first pieced together from clues. It is *disclosed* — an
angel descends from the sky and says it out loud, and the earthquake, the lightning-appearance, the
snow-white clothing are the standard grammar of a biblical theophany (a scene in which God, or God's
messenger, breaks visibly into the world — think of Sinai). Matthew's second warrant is **testimony**:
once the news is disclosed, it travels by word of mouth — "go quickly and tell," and then the women
"ran to bring his disciples word." Revelation first, then testimony. Something is shown; then it is
told.

Matthew is also the gospel that argues back. In a short, strange scene the guards report to the chief
priests, who pay them to say the disciples stole the body while they slept — and Matthew notes that this
counter-story "was spread abroad among the Jews, and continues until today." The text is not naïve about
its own claim; it knows there is a rival explanation in circulation, and it names it in order to answer
it. Read in the world of the gospel's own composition — the years after 70, when Jewish followers of
Jesus and the emerging rabbinic movement were arguing over the same inheritance — the little scene reads
like a trace of exactly that argument, each side accusing the other of getting the story wrong.[^matt-stolen-body]

The reception history hears all of this. Justin Martyr, writing in the second century in one of the
earliest surviving reflections on resurrection (*On the Resurrection*), takes up the bodily claim that
Matthew's angel makes flatly — *he is not here* — and defends it as a claim about the body, not a
metaphor for the survival of a soul. And far downstream, Howard Thurman, preaching Easter to a
twentieth-century Black congregation, hears in exactly this vindication-of-the-crushed what Matthew's
first readers heard: that the one the world's authorities condemned is the one God raised — good news
addressed, first and pointedly, to people the world does not credit.

## The comparative lens

Now step back and look at all four at once, because the differences are not noise. They are four
answers to a single quiet question: *how do you know the tomb is empty?*

Two of the gospels answer with **revelation** — the news arrives announced. Matthew and Mark both open
their resurrection scenes with a messenger who simply *tells* the women what has happened, before anyone
has weighed a single piece of evidence. The angel speaks; the fact lands from above.

The other two answer with **testimony** — the news arrives as report, carried by a witness who was
there. Luke and John lead not with a voice from the sky but with someone who saw, and went, and said so.
The knowing travels human to human; the reader is asked to trust a witness.

And then John does something none of the others do. Its Frame records a second commitment the others
lack — a commitment to the **material**, the physical remains. John lingers on the grave-cloths: the
linen wrappings lying where the body had been, and — a detail no other gospel keeps — the cloth that had
covered the head, "not lying with the other linen cloths, but rolled up in a place by itself." John
wants you to look at the folded cloth. The empty tomb, in John, is not only announced and not only
witnessed; it is *inspected*. There is evidence you could have held in your hands.

So: two gospels where the truth descends, two where it is passed hand to hand, and one of those two that
stops to show you the folded linen. A reader can hold the whole comparison in a single image — an angel
speaking from a stone, and a folded cloth lying in the dark — and that image is the difference.

There is one honest footnote to Mark's ending that belongs here, because it bears on how Mark answers the
question at all. The earliest and best manuscripts of Mark end at 16:8 — with the women fleeing the tomb
and saying "nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." No appearance of the risen Jesus; just terror and
an empty tomb. The fuller ending printed in most Bibles (Mark 16:9–20), including the one this room quotes
from, is a **later addition** — the early church found Mark's abrupt stop unbearable and supplied a
resolution. Scholars distinguish among the manuscript endings — a *shorter* ending and a *longer* one
(the 16:9–20 printed above); the coded Sitz names this added material the **Intermediate Ending**. It is
worth knowing that the earliest gospel may have ended not with a triumphant appearance but with an open, frightened
silence — and that later hands could not leave it there.
