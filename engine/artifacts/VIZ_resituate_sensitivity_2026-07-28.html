We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of all things visible and invisible. And in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, begotten of the Father, the only-begotten; that is, of the essence of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance (homoousion) with the Father, by whom all things were made both in heaven and on earth; who for us men and for our salvation came down and was incarnate and was made man; he suffered, and the third day he rose again, ascended into heaven; from thence he shall come to judge the quick and the dead. The historian’s objection. The reconstruction treats 'no emperor' as if it also meant 'no Alexander of Alexandria's prior regional condemnation of Arius' — but the Arian controversy was already a live intra-episcopal fight with anathemas flying before Constantine intervened, so a purely ecclesiastical council with the same personnel would almost certainly still issue targeted anti-Arian language, just without the universal-binding homoousian test-word; the generator's total VANISH of M20-M31 overshoots.

grounded
  • M1-M6 and M11-M19 surviving as scriptural/narrative baptismal substrate is well-attested across pre-Nicene regional creeds (Old Roman, Interrogatory creeds in Hippolytus, Eastern baptismal formulae).
  • The claim that homoousios (M10) was contested and actively avoided in the post-325 Eusebian/Homoian trajectory (Antioch 341 'Dedication', Sirmium, Nike/Ariminum 359-360) is a mainstream reading in Hanson, Ayres, and Behr.
  • The observation that pre-Constantinian councils (Carthage, Elvira, Antioch pre-325) issued regional canons without a mechanism to bind the whole Church to a single christological test-word is historically accurate.
  • Treating the anathemas (M20-M31) as dependent on a coercive enforcement mechanism is defensible — Arius and Secundus/Theonas were in fact exiled by imperial action, not by episcopal power alone.
fan-fiction risk
  • The perturbed creed's closing clause ('Each church holds this faith according to the Scriptures received from the apostles, as its bishops in council have handed it down') is a modern-sounding meta-rubric with no fourth-century credal parallel; real pre-Nicene creeds do not editorialize about their own regional status.
  • M9 (begotten-not-made) is asserted to VANISH, but the begotten/made distinction has pre-Nicene scriptural and apologetic roots (e.g., in Origen and in Alexander of Alexandria's own encyclical prior to imperial involvement); its total disappearance is stronger than the documented forces warrant — WEAKEN would be safer than VANISH.
  • The counterfactual under-weights that even without an emperor, Alexander of Alexandria had already excommunicated Arius regionally (~318-320); a council held 'on bishops' own account' with the Alexandrian party present would likely still produce SOME anti-Arian language, not a wholesale VANISH of M20-M31.

Generator honesty note. The strongest temptation was to have this council explicitly endorse a subordinationist Christology (a positively Arian-compatible clause), since the 350s Homoian creeds drifted that way. I resisted: the documented pre-Constantinian pattern is regional DIVERSITY and scriptural reticence, not a positive counter-definition — so the perturbed creed leaves the relation underspecified rather than tilting it.

He inclined his head in grave Latin courtesy, received the message the deacon carried, and answered him aloud — pitched loudly enough for those nearest to hear, though formally addressed only to the deacon: > *"Tell my brother Alexander that I have not come the length of the empire to speak with him first and the others afterward. Tell him I will come to him within the hour, and that I hope by then to come as one member of a synod, not as a guest in his party. Ask him, in charity, to wait for me at the basilica door."* He watched the deacon go. Alexander, across the courtyard at the basilica door, lowered his previously raised hand. Hosius then caught the eye of his own deacon — the one man of his traveling clergy still with him — and spoke to him quickly and privately in Latin: > *"Go to Eustathius of Antioch. Say only this: 'The bishop of Corduba asks whether Eustathius would consent to be named presider, if the name were put forward now, from the steps.' Bring me his answer in one word. Then find Caecilian of Carthage and tell him to come and stand at the foot of these steps, visibly, within the next few minutes. Go."* His deacon slipped into the crowd on both commissions. Hosius did not move from the steps. He waited, standing still, and let his gaze travel — deliberately, without hurry — across the forecourt: to the eastern colonnade, where Arius was still speaking to his growing circle; to the basilica door, where Alexander now waited; to the outer arch, where the two Roman priest-legates remained huddled together. He held his silhouette on the steps — old, staff in hand, plainly Western, plainly alone — and let it work on the crowd for him. When his deacon returned with Eustathius's one-word answer, and when Caecilian of Carthage had come and taken his place, visibly, at the foot of the steps, Hosius stepped up onto the highest of the western steps and raised his staff for silence. He spoke first briefly in Latin, so that the Roman legates would hear him named and bear witness that Rome's oldest ally in the East had spoken; then, more slowly and more carefully, in Greek, so that the assembly at large would hear him. He named no doctrine. He did not name Arius. He did not name *homoousios*. He said, in substance: > *"Brothers and fellow-bishops. We are here. No one has called us; the question has called us. Before we can answer it we must first be a synod, and not a crowd. I therefore propose, as the eldest Western bishop present and as one who bears the greeting of the bishop of Rome, three things, and only three. First: that we constitute ourselves, from this hour, as a council of the whole church, and that each bishop present be enrolled by see and by province before the sun sets today. Second: that we ask our brother Eustathius of Antioch, whose see is apostolic and whose person is known to us all, to preside; and that the bishops of Alexandria and of Nicomedia sit at his right and his left, so that no party may say it was excluded from the chair. Third: that our first act, when we convene within the basilica after the midday meal, be the reading — in full, and in their own words — of the letter of our brother Alexander of Alexandria and the letter of the presbyter Arius, so that this synod hears the dispute from the disputants and not from their friends. On the question itself I will say nothing today. I ask only for these three things, that we may become a body capable of answering it."* With the motion on the air, he looked deliberately first to Eustathius of Antioch and then to Caecilian of Carthage — cueing seconds from East and West within the same breath. Then he stepped down from the steps, crossed the forecourt without hurry to Alexander at the basilica door, took the older man's arm as an equal, and went in with him to the midday meal. **What happened as a result.** Hosius refused the private Egyptian audience without giving offense; his refusal was heard by every bishop within earshot on the steps. He converted a milling crowd, on the edge of scattering for its midday meal, into a synod: the assembly constituted itself as a council of the whole church, agreed to be enrolled by see and by province before sundown, and accepted Eustathius of Antioch as presider — with Alexander of Alexandria and Eusebius of Nicomedia seated at his right and his left, so that neither party could later claim exclusion. It agreed that its first substantive act, after the midday meal, would be the reading in full of Alexander's letter and of Arius's letter, from the disputants' own hands. And by walking arm-in-arm with Alexander into the meal — plainly, in view of the whole courtyard — Hosius signaled that he was Alexander's brother, but not Alexander's man; the Latin, procedurally-minded elder from Corduba had made himself the convening figure the assembly did not otherwise possess.
