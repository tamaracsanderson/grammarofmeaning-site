Grammar of Meaning

What a text is made of

An HDS (Harvard Divinity School) thesis proposal — a theory of how meaning is made, and a method that makes the theory testable. It is offered to religious studies, tested first on scripture and then on ordinary secular texts to show the method is a property of the instrument, not of any one tradition.

The problem it answers. Much close reading of a sacred text treats it as a container: the meaning is inside, and interpretation is the work of drawing it out. That approach handles a single settled reading well, but it leaves the questions the field actually argues about — why two traditions read the same passage in opposite ways, what a text's silences are doing, why one variant matters — resting on the interpreter's judgment, with no shared way to check one reading against another.

The theory. A text is not a container; it is a set of moves — small, named actions, each recorded in the same few slots: who does what, to whom, and what the action leaves unsaid. What a text means depends on what you recompose those moves into: hold the moves still and turn them a different way, and a different meaning comes into view. The instrument recomposes a text in four ways — the silence-scan runs across everything it has coded; the other three are so far pilot runs on particular texts — and each yields a reading you can contest at a specific move rather than in general:

  • its silences — what a move raises but never answers; a scan the instrument runs on every text it codes.
  • the alternatives it could have been — the engine generates the other moves the text could have made in a given spot, keeps only the ones that stay coherent, and reads the actual move as a choice against those alternatives. In a pilot on the line "She Loves You," it has done this 36 times: the meaning of the line is what it rules out — not reporting your own feelings, not "she doesn't feel for you." The point is not the opposite word; it is that the choice, and its cost, become explicit and reproducible.
  • the readings history gave it — how later readers recomposed the same moves (a pilot run on Paul's letter to the Romans, chapter 8).
  • its variants across tellings — the same moment told more than once, differently (the four canonical gospels each narrate the empty tomb, and their differences are data, not error).

Four recompositions, four readings, one text — not ranked, and not always in agreement. Each is a reading you can check at a named move, which is what distinguishes them from a matter of taste.

The method is what makes those readings checkable, and it is built, not promised. Every text is read the same way: a move grammar (each move filed in the fixed slots above — agent, action, object, and what is left unsaid), seven coordinates that place the text (among them: on what grounds it claims to know, what it is for, and what it takes to be real), and gates that refuse a reading the evidence will not carry — where the evidence is the text's own moves plus the agreement of independent readers who never see each other's answers. It runs as a reproducible pipeline: one text, walked stage by stage, each stage a self-contained step whose input, operation, and output are all named and open to inspection. A stranger can re-run it and disagree specifically, at a named move, instead of in general. What the method is, and what it has actually been run on, are kept as separate claims on separate pages — the claim should not carry numbers that change every time a job finishes.

Why it is unusual. The project's two neighbours each hold half of it. Interpretation theory, in its dominant forms, offers an account of meaning but no shared, replicable protocol for producing a reading; computational text-analysis offers a replicable protocol but no theory anchored to the individual interpretive move. A theory with no method is a claim you can only assent to; a method with no theory of meaning can process a text without ever claiming to have read it. Joining them is the contribution: a claim about how meaning is made that a stranger can reproduce, test, and contest, move by move.
