Grammar of Meaning

What a text is made of

This is an HDS thesis proposal — a theory and a method offered to religious studies. The theory: a text is not a container you extract meaning from. It is a set of moves, and what it means depends on what you recompose those moves into — the alternatives it could have been, commentary on its silences, the readings history gave it, its variants across tellings. Four recompositions, four meanings, one text. The method is what makes that checkable: seven coordinates, a move grammar, gates, and a run anyone can reproduce and disagree with specifically. Theory alone is a claim you can only assent to. Method alone is machinery with nothing at stake. Together: a claim about meaning that can be tested — which is the contribution, and the part that is genuinely unusual. And the method turns on itself. Run on its own method-statement, the instrument surfaced a commitment it had left unsaid: that every output must read from the coded object rather than the original text, installing the coding as the authoritative layer with no argued warrant. Two forebears from opposite frames — Genette’s transtextuality, Kugel’s reception-history — independently converged on the same objection. The remedy is a familiar one, a single store of record; what is unusual is that the method surfaced it about itself. That the instrument can find, name, and let its own forebears adjudicate its own unstated commitments is part of what it is. And the reader here is a system. Each recomposition is itself a reading, and reader-response theory holds that meaning is made in the reading. But the reader doing the recomposing is not a person: it is the instrument — a person and an AI, each bringing a frame to the coded object. The four meanings are not neutral extractions; they are the readings this hybrid reader performs, which is why naming the reader is part of the method.
