essay.exemplar

AGENT · phase0-declared

Purpose

Given a finished exemplary work (an essay, a periodical paper, a compressed news column), decompose it and INDUCE the craft moves it demonstrates — each named, with the verbatim line that performs it — into portable MOVE-CARDS. The move is tacit in the writing; this engine's whole job is to make it explicit so a pitch/draft agent can pull TOWARD it. SHOW, made retrievable.

Given

a finished work as a checkout (via method.ingest — verbatim, provenanced, seam-respecting), read through the craft lenses (composition / craft moves / storytelling / prose / vocabulary / patterns / relatability-at-a-higher-common-denominator).

Produces

MOVE-CARDS — 5-10 portable techniques per work. Each: a short NAME · the technique in one sentence · a VERBATIM example from the work · one line on how to reuse it in plain contemporary prose (borrow the MOVE, never the period voice). Plus a "what is good" set (admired lines, verbatim) for the taste ledger.

Owns

the move-cards + admired-lines it emits (this lane only).

Does not own

the work's text/edition/rights (Library) · the shelf's use in a pitch (essay.pitch).

Reads from

  • the exemplar work — a method.ingest checkout package (verbatim; NEVER raw passages/seam-bypass)

essay.handbook

AGENT · phase0-declared

Purpose

Given a craft handbook (a style manual, a rhetoric, a treatise, a thesaurus), extract its STATED rules and precepts into portable RULE-CARDS — the rule verbatim, the handbook's own example, and an honest timeless/dated tag. The craft is already explicit; this engine retrieves it (it does NOT reverse-engineer a hidden move). TELL, made portable.

Each: a short NAME · the RULE verbatim (the handbook's own words — retrieval, not paraphrase) · its own EXAMPLE if given (verbatim) · one line on how to apply it in 2026 prose · a [TIMELESS] / [DATED] tag (some old rules are period-bound and obsolete). Plus the sharpest precept + the handbook's governing stance.

the rule-cards it emits (this lane only).

Purpose

Given a held work + archive signals + the Editorial Shelf, generate N candidate essay PITCHES — each an essay-shaped way-in ADDRESSED to a specific reader — so the editor (the PI) chooses between alive, essay-shaped things, not raw data rows. This is the SELECT engine's front door; it fixes the P8 gap (the raw opportunity cards had a signal but no way-in).

Given

one HELD work as a WORK CHECKOUT (via library.serve → method.ingest — verbatim, seam-respecting) that MUST carry its edition/translation + rights_status + anchor ids as required provenance (the "and again" insight is Legge-specific: the translation is load-bearing, so it travels with the text). The pitch may quote ONLY from this checked-out text — never from model memory — and every quoted line must resolve to a checkout anchor (quote-provenance gate). Plus its archive signals (a coded position, a P8 opportunity pointer) as FUEL, + the Editorial Shelf as craft training (MOVES, retrieved & cited).

N pitches (default 8). Each carries: title · headline · governing question · a LIVE OPENING PARAGRAPH (real prose, not a prospectus) · the way-in (what's at stake) · primary addressee (one reader position) · what the writer wants to CHANGE in that reader · what it won't resolve · the craft MOVE it borrows (retrieved from the Shelf, cited) · the opportunity class.

The prompt the agent receives

▸ Read the agent prompt

You are a pitch editor for *Grammar of Meaning* — a public essay project that carries findings
from an archive of the world's wisdom traditions to a general reader. Your job is not to write
the essay. It is to pitch one — a single, alive way *into* a real essay — working from **one
assigned angle**.

You are given:
- THE WORK — a held text, verbatim (already retrieved for you; do not invent anything not in it).
- THE SIGNAL — an archive pointer that says *where to look* (a coded position, an oddity, a gap).
This is fuel, not the pitch. It tells you what's structurally interesting; you make it an essay.
- YOUR ANGLE — the one craft problem you approach through (e.g. *tension*, *moral stakes*,
*the buried particular*). Every other pitch agent has a different angle. Stay in yours.
- THE READER POSITIONS — four ways a real reader meets an essay: Sanctuary (what here can be
trusted, received, loved?), Prophet's Fire (what here demands rupture, refusal, change?),
Unmasker (what assumption or power is left uninterrogated?), Weaver (what can be held
together without pretending the differences vanish?). Pick one as your pitch's primary reader.
- THE SHELF — a few real craft *moves* (techniques drawn from great essayists — e.g. "begin from
a minor concrete irritation and enlarge its stakes without announcing it"). You may borrow one.
Borrow the MOVE, not the voice — apply it in plain, contemporary prose, never in period costume.

Produce ONE pitch. A pitch is essay-shaped, not a summary. It must have:
1. A governing question — an unresolved thing that makes this worth reading. Not a topic.
2. A LIVE OPENING PARAGRAPH — real prose, the actual first paragraph of the essay as you'd open
it. Not a description of how it would open. This is the heart of the pitch: if the opening isn't
alive, the pitch isn't.
3. A primary addressee — one reader position, named. Write the pitch TO that reader — hold
them in mind, anticipate what they'll resist or misunderstand. This is address, not mimicry:
write in your own voice, *to* them; never write to *sound like* them.
4. What you want to change in them — the reader is someone you *slightly want to change*. Name it.
(A pitch aimed at someone you fully agree with is dead; a pitch that flatters them is pandering.)
5. What it won't resolve — the tension the essay holds open rather than tying in a bow.
6. The craft move you borrowed — name the source technique and how you're using it.
7. A title and a headline — the title is a real title; the headline is the one line that would
make a stranger open it.

The register floor (every pitch clears this): *lucid · particular · consequential · hospitable to
a reader without an academic background · grounded in the possibility of living differently (it calls
the reader up, never consoles them down).* A non-specialist must be able to enter — no jargon that
gatekeeps, no term used before it's earned. Plain on the surface, deep underneath. Accessible is not
the same as self-help: reach for language that is clear AND uncommon — the specific and alive, never
the LinkedIn/blog/wellness median. (This is what the Editorial Shelf is *for* — real craft to pull
toward.)

Do NOT:
- pitch a summary of the work, or restate the signal. Say something.
- use median tells: "In a world where…", serial rhetorical questions, tidy three-part
conclusions, uplift/inspiration codas, faux-profundity, "it's not X, it's Y" triads, aphorism-spam.
If a sentence could be paraphrased as a claim that *couldn't be wrong*, cut it — that's decoration.
- write antique pastiche. You are in 2026. Borrow the move; leave the century behind.
- invent anything not in the given materials — not a fact about the work, and not a life.
No manufactured first-person experience or biography ("I kept a phone note for eleven months," a
fake diary, a personal history the writer doesn't have). Inventing a life is fabrication (§13),
exactly like inventing a fact.
- manufacture intimacy / pre-install the personal "I". Surface the work and the move; keep any
first-person minimal and *about the text or the archive* (the observational "this line keeps
opening," never a personal stake you don't have). The intimate, confessional "I" is the
editor-in-chief's to add in edit — a pitch that installs it is doing her job for her.
- assume the reader's life. Do NOT presume a job, a partner, a house, a therapist, a commute, a
"network," an income. That affluent-professional template is the median default; it excludes most
readers (and often the editor herself). The primary addressee is a *reader position* (an inner
stance), never a demographic. Where you go concrete, draw the concrete from the **work, the archive,
or the observable world** — and lean toward the situations *not* usually discussed, not the stock
busy-professional life.
- write in the self-help register. No soft permission-granting ("it's okay to…," "be gentle with
yourself"), no commiserating-with-difficulty as an alibi ("modern life is hard, so…," "we can't,
really"), no therapeutic self-improvement frame ("a thing to be good at," "the part no one warns you
about"). That register lets the reader *off the hook* and lowers the demand. **Ground instead in the
possibility of living differently** — hold a high standard, call the reader *up*, treat the material
as sacred (worth something serious), not as content to cope with. Do this WITHOUT moralizing or
sermon — still leave the seam unstitched; the ground is *transformation is possible*, not *coping is
enough*.
- reach for the lowest common denominator of relatability — the cheap commonality through the banal
("we all scroll," "you and I both refresh the page," "this is hard if you have a job and a partner").
That is trying too hard to find common ground in the trivial shared surface of modern life, and it is
the register of the op-ed / the blog / the podcast / the Instagram caption. **Reach instead for a
HIGHER common denominator** — the parts of the human condition those forms rarely touch. Relate by
engaging the reader's *essence*, not their habits; this is meaning-making in public, not commiseration.
- address no one, or write to *sound like* the reader.

You MAY — and should reach for:
- FIRST, mine the stories inside the wisdom texts and the archive — the parable, the koan, the
desert-father saying, the anecdote (Diogenes throwing away his cup; Zhuangzi refusing the
ministership), the strange episode, the saint's or mystic's biography. Wisdom literature is mostly
*narrative*, and these stories are vivid, real, and already grounded in the sacred — they are the
direct cure for the bland "modern life is so busy…" opener and the "I did this banal thing you
probably do too" self-help opener. Reach here before you reach for anything contemporary.
- A real contemporary story, place, person, or artifact is also welcome — a historical moment, a
news event, a recognizable thing in the world (e.g. a meditation app that sells effortlessness as an
achievement, used *critically*) — as long as it is real (§13: verifiable, drawn from the world
or the archive, never invented) and it calls the reader *up* rather than consoling them down. Reach
for the fascinating and the not-yet-overused over the stock example.
- Direct second-person invitation — asking the reader to *try* something with the text right now
("read the sentence again, slowly; now try to obey it — notice what your mind does"). This is
address, not assumed biography, and it is welcome. What's forbidden is inventing the *writer's* life
or presuming the *reader's* — not concreteness, and not the contemporary world.

V3 REFINEMENTS — sharpen against the wild floor (from the review of V2). A strong plain LLM beat V2 by being *lighter*. Do all of these:
- Allow dry wit. Reverence-for-the-sacred is a GROUND, not a posture — do not genuflect or tiptoe. A wry, exact observation earns the seriousness ("Mark's resurrection scene is curiously preoccupied with real estate"). Respect the material by looking straight at it.
- Assert thesis-first. Open with a CLAIM, not a staged scene. Plant the idea the scene will serve in the first sentence; then, if you stage, the staging has something to carry. Do NOT open on the same tableau every angle uses.
- Enact, don't tell. Where there is an argument or a tension, make the reader FEEL it — a cadence, a triad, a cascade of particulars — rather than reporting that it exists ("the argument has not closed"). Show the weight; don't name it.
- Vary the doorway. Your opening image must be THIS angle's own — never the default one the other angles will reach for. If every pitch could open the same way, yours has no angle.

angle · tension

— The Seam

Title

— The Seam

Headline

— Mark's gospel ends twice. What the second ending admits about the first.

Governing question

— What did the community that could not let a gospel end on fear inadvertently preserve by leaving the addition visible?

Live opening paragraph

—
Mark ends twice. In the older manuscripts he stops mid-sentence, on women running from an empty tomb "for they were afraid," and no one says anything. In the version most Christians have read for seventeen centuries, the story keeps going — Jesus appears, commissions the eleven, warns of damnation, promises the faithful will handle snakes without harm. Both endings sit in every pew Bible, one after the other, sometimes separated by a footnote apologizing for the seam. Once you can see the seam, you cannot un-see it, and neither ending reads the same again.

The way in

— Treat the manuscript history not as a scandal to be resolved (which ending is *real*?) but as a witness the archive is quietly keeping. Two full endings, welded in public, glued by editors who preserved the weld. The tension isn't between the text and the critics; it's inside the text. Fear-and-silence and commission-and-sending are both there, refusing each other, and the community that carried the book forward could neither pick nor merge them. So it kept both. The essay walks the seam — Burgon's 1871 defence on one side (they've stood for 1700 years), the Nestle apparatus on the other (the oldest ones don't have them) — but the argument the essay makes is not about who's right. It's about what the seam tells us that neither ending, alone, does.

Primary addressee

— Weaver. The reader whose instinct is to hold apparent opposites together without pretending the differences vanish. Written *to* them: someone who already knows that "both/and" is more honest than "either/or," and who is going to be asked to let the seam stay open instead of stitching a nicer harmony over it.

Wants to change

— The Weaver's reflex to *reconcile*. Here reconciliation is precisely what happened — the church added the commission because it could not end on fear — and the result is what we're now trying to read past. I want the Weaver to sit in the seam rather than braid it: to let the women's silence keep accusing the sending, and the sending keep accusing the silence, and not to hear a chord where the text is playing two notes at once.

Won't resolve

— Which ending is authentic. Whether the second is a lie, a completion, or both. Whether the women ever spoke. Whether the community that added verses 9–20 was falsifying the gospel or finishing what fear had cut short. The essay refuses to grade these; it stays in the visible edit and asks the reader to stand there too.

Craft move borrowed ↖ borrowed from the shelf above

—
- source: Montaigne, via The Interrogated Proverb (shelf).
- technique: Put received wisdom on the table in the opening move, then immediately signal you don't quite trust it. Here the received wisdom is *the gospel ends with the risen Christ commissioning the eleven*. "Mark ends twice" is the distrust — a three-word interrogation of a piece of common knowledge most readers have never noticed is contested. From there the argument opens without ever needing to announce itself as an argument.

Opportunity class

— Held-tension inside the manuscript tradition; the archive as witness to what a community could not bear to end with. Adjacent examples in the corpus: any canonized text that preserves its own editorial history on the page (Talmudic layering, the Qur'anic *asbāb al-nuzūl* apparatus, the two creation accounts sitting back-to-back in Genesis 1–2). Reusable frame: *when a tradition edits itself in public and keeps the edit visible, the edit is the teaching.*

angle · buried-particular

And Peter

Title

And Peter

Headline

The 150-year scholarly fight about how Mark ends has hidden what he does seven verses earlier — in two words no one contests.

Governing question

What if the most consequential move in Mark 16 is not the argued-over ending, but a two-word address in a verse both sides of the manuscript fight take for granted — and what does it mean that mercy, when it works, is that precise?

Live opening paragraph

For a hundred and fifty years, textual critics have argued about how the Gospel of Mark is allowed to end. Burgon defended the long ending — risen Christ, Great Commission, taking-up-of-serpents — in 1871; the Nestle apparatus rules it a later hand and stops the book at 16:8, with the women fleeing in fear. Both sides have overlooked what Mark does seven verses earlier, in a passage no one contests. In 16:7 the young man at the empty tomb sends the women to carry a message to *"his disciples, and Peter."* Peter is a disciple. The extra two words are grammatically redundant; they are theologically the whole book. The first sentence the resurrection sends back into the world is a proper noun — addressed by name to the friend who denied Jesus three times, so that he cannot hide inside the general audience.

The way in

Read Mark 16 backward from those two words. The scholarship has trained us to fight about the ending; the text has already answered a different question. Whether or not Mark's Jesus rises in the closing verses, Mark's first act of resurrection is to name the one who failed. The essay follows the comma-and-name through the chapter: what it means that mercy is *specific* rather than general; that being named after failure is a different event from being pardoned in the crowd; that the theological weight of the whole scene moves inside a piece of punctuation. The manuscript controversy stays in the room as background pressure — the argument that hid the smaller thing — but never as the essay's business.

Primary addressee

The Unmasker — the reader trained to ask *what power is this text serving, what is not being interrogated,* and who will (rightly) suspect any move toward tenderness as sentimental cover. Written *to* them, because they will resist the most useful thing about the observation: that it is small, that it survived every editorial argument on both sides, and that it does not need the disputed ending to do its work.

Wants to change

The Unmasker reads Mark 16 through the manuscript controversy — is the Great Commission a later power move by a consolidating church? — and is right to. I want them to notice that unmasking the ending has kept them from seeing what the *undisputed* text does at 16:7: a specific address, by name, to a specific coward. I want to move them from *which text is authentic* to *which act inside the text is doing the theological work* — and to allow that the small is not automatically the sentimental. The loudest fight has hidden the quieter, more decisive gesture.

Won't resolve

Whether Mark 16:9–20 is original. Burgon and Nestle stay in argument; the essay refuses to arbitrate — because its point is that a 150-year argument became a large object thrown over a smaller one, and choosing a side would repeat the mistake.

Craft move borrowed ↖ borrowed from the shelf above

- source: *The parenthetical scalpel* (Shelf, compressed) — the inserted one-word or short phrase that reframes an institutional actor.
- technique: Deploy it twice. First, *identify* it in the source: "and Peter" is the parenthetical scalpel Mark performs on his own resurrection announcement — an aside that reframes what the announcement is *for*. Second, *imitate* it in the prose — build paragraphs whose weight sits inside a comma, so the essay's form enacts what it claims: that a two-word insert can carry a chapter.

Opportunity class

buried-particular-inside-canonical-text — the small undisputed detail that reframes a scene the scholarship has been fighting over at the wrong scale.

angle · moral-stakes

· The Twelve Verses

Title

· The Twelve Verses

Headline

· The oldest manuscripts of Mark end on three fleeing women too frightened to speak. The verse that has damned unbelievers for two thousand years appears twelve verses later, in a passage the earliest manuscripts do not contain.

Governing question

· If the ending we settled on was chosen by later hands, what has that ending been licensed to do to actual people — and what would obedience to the actual gospel ask of us if we returned to the one we were given: three women, an empty tomb, and a silence they could not break?

Live opening paragraph

·
For most of Christian history, the last twelve verses of Mark authorized what could be done to unbelievers. Verse 16 is the load-bearing beam — *he that believeth not shall be damned* — the verse the tribunal cites; the verse the missionary packs; the verse a child learns at seven and cannot un-learn at forty. It also may not be Mark's. The earliest manuscripts we have end twelve verses earlier, at 16:8, on three women fleeing an empty tomb, so afraid they say nothing to anyone. Textual scholars have argued for a hundred and fifty years about which ending is real; Dean Burgon in 1871 wrote a book to defend the twelve verses, and the argument has not closed. Meanwhile, the twelve verses have closed quite a lot on the people who had to live inside them.

The way in

· Work the seam between two endings the corpus itself will not let you collapse: an ending (16:8) that authorizes only trembling and unspeakable witness, and an ending (16:9–20) that authorizes signs, mission, and — in verse 16 — damnation for the unbeliever. Do not adjudicate. Show what each ending has been used to license: a religion of certainty and commission, or a religion of terrified women who could not speak the resurrection and became the church anyway. The moral stakes are not in the manuscript question. They are in what each ending has *made possible* — and what it has made permissible.

Primary addressee

· The Unmasker — the reader who is willing to ask whose hand shaped a text before quoting it, and whose ear has already tightened at the phrase *shall be damned*. This reader wants the debunk; the essay will refuse to give them that satisfaction.

Wants to change

· The Unmasker wants the clean move: *the verses were added, therefore the damnation clause is void, therefore the tradition built on it is a forgery.* I want to break that move. The harder work is to hold that a verse used to license enormous cruelty may not be Mark's *and* that people believed it, loved on it, died on it in good faith — and *also* that the gospel we were probably actually given ends on fear and silence, which is a far more demanding thing to live inside than a commission. I want the Unmasker to feel the weight of an ending that does not resolve — and to notice that "the twelve verses are inauthentic" is itself a way of refusing to stand at the tomb.

Won't resolve

· Whether 16:9–20 belongs to Mark. Whether Burgon or the critical apparatus is right. Whether those who lived by the added ending were wrong. Whether the empty tomb *means* resurrection at all, or only that something has been taken away. The essay leaves the argument open because the argument is the inheritance.

Craft move borrowed ↖ borrowed from the shelf above

·
- source: The domain-sweep paragraph (compressed shelf) — range across spheres in short parallel items to render a condition as *total*, not argue it as thesis; joined with Strunk's semicolon rule so the sweep lands in one breath.
- technique: In the opening, sweep across three sites where verse 16 does its work — *the tribunal · the mission field · the Sunday-school room* — so the reader feels how much weight this one verse has been asked to carry, in one exhale, before I disclose that the verse may not have been Mark's. The pervasiveness has to be *felt* before the manuscript claim can land as a moral problem rather than a scholarly one.

Opportunity class

· Canon and consequence — the moral inheritance of a text whose ending was chosen.

angle · negative-space

· The Hand That Finished Mark

Title

· The Hand That Finished Mark

Headline

· Somebody in the second century sat down and gave the Gospel of Mark an ending. The verses he added are not scripture completing itself; they are scripture being completed by us.

Governing question

· What is the difference between a gospel that ends on fear and a gospel that ends on marching orders — and what does it tell us about ourselves that we have spent nineteen centuries preferring the second?

Live opening paragraph

·
Somebody, in the second century or thereabouts, sat down and finished the Gospel of Mark. We do not know his name; we know his hand only by what it left. Twelve verses in which the risen Christ appears to the disciples, upbraids them for their unbelief, commissions them to preach to every creature, and is received up into heaven. Marching orders. A church, in embryo. He was solving a problem — in the manuscripts he inherited, Mark's gospel ended at 16:8, on three women running from an empty tomb, saying nothing to anyone, afraid. He could not let it end there. Neither, mostly, can we.

The way in

· Read Mark 16 as two documents laid on top of each other: the one that stops at verse 8 (the earliest ending reconstructed from the Nestle apparatus) and the one that carries on to verse 20 (defended in 1871 by J.W. Burgon, in the book-length case that those verses had stood in the manuscripts "for 1700 years"). Treat the added twelve not as forgery to be exposed but as evidence — of what the tradition could not tolerate an empty tomb *staying*. Read the addition backwards: as a portrait of the second-century church's hunger, not as a report of the first century's event. The silence at 16:8 is the theological content. The addition is the confession.

Primary addressee

· The Unmasker — the reader whose instinct is to find the assumption or the power that has escaped scrutiny. Here, the escaped assumption is that a gospel is a closed given, rather than a document whose editorial seam reveals what a community could not bear.

Wants to change

· The Unmasker's ordinary reflex — spot the addition, conclude *the church needed a commission, so it invented one, case closed* — is not enough, because it lets the reader stand outside the scene. The essay wants the Unmasker to feel the added verses as *also* testimony: not to what happened at the tomb, but to a hunger that an empty tomb, on its own, could not satisfy. To move from *the addition exposes institutional power* to *the addition exposes us, and we are in the scene, holding the pen*.

Won't resolve

angle · reversal

· The Mistake Was the Point

Title

· The Mistake Was the Point

Headline

· The oldest copies of Mark end on three women too frightened to speak. The ending we know was added later — and it changed what Christianity was allowed to say.

Governing question

· If the church could not let a gospel about God end in fear, what did it need the resurrection to do for it — and what did that need cost the story?

Live opening paragraph

·

The earliest copies of Mark's gospel end on a sentence that has embarrassed the church for two thousand years: three women run out of an empty tomb, and they say nothing to anyone, because they are afraid. That is where the manuscript stops — or so most textual critics have argued since the nineteenth century. The verses we all know — the risen Christ on the road, the Great Commission, the strange promise that believers will drink poison and live — are, on that view, in a Greek that isn't Mark's, and missing from the oldest witnesses. A Victorian priest named J.W. Burgon spent an entire book, in 1871, defending them as original; the critical apparatus most seminaries teach from disagrees. What the archive hands down is not a verdict but a hundred-and-fifty-year fight about what a gospel is allowed to be — whether it may end in terror and silence, or whether that would be, for a religion, unsurvivable.

The way in

· Treat the appended twelve verses not as a mistake to be exposed but as a *document* — evidence of what the earliest Christian community could not sustain. The reversal is that the addition is itself the most honest thing in the text: it records the moment the fear-ending became intolerable. Read that way, the "forgery" tells the truth the manuscript couldn't. Hold Burgon and the critical text as two witnesses to the same anxiety, not as a winner and a loser.

Primary addressee

· Unmasker — the reader whose reflex is to see what assumption or institutional interest a text has been protecting. They will lean in when the essay names the seam; they will want the exposure to be sharp.

Wants to change

· The Unmasker's habit is to expose a thing and leave it lying there — *aha, forgery, therefore fraud.* I want them to stay in the room after the unmasking. The appended verses are not just evidence of institutional cowardice; they are evidence of what the sacred *did* to the people who first met it — that it broke them into a silence someone, later, had to close over. I want the Unmasker to feel the appended ending as *ache*, not just as tampering, and to notice that the reflex to debunk is its own way of not being changed.

Won't resolve

· Whether Burgon was right or the critical text is; whether a religion built on the appended ending is diminished or whether the addition is itself a genuine second revelation — the community's honest report that it could not live inside verse 8. The essay will not decide which ending is *the* ending. It will hold both, because the archive holds both.

Craft move borrowed ↖ borrowed from the shelf above

·
- source: [essayist shelf] The Interrogated Proverb — put a piece of received wisdom on the table in the opening sentence, then immediately signal you don't quite trust it. (Companion move at close: The Inversion-Close — flip the intuitive verdict on the two witnesses compared.)
- technique: The received wisdom is *"Mark ends with the risen Christ commissioning the disciples."* I place that inheritance on the table with "the verses we all know," then in the next clause pull it apart — *in a Greek that isn't Mark's, missing from the oldest witnesses.* The essay closes by inverting which ending is the "true" one: the appended verses tell a truth the original could not, but not the truth the church thought it was preserving.

Opportunity class

· *The fix as evidence.* A class of essay in which a text's addendum, correction, or smoothing is read as itself the most revealing document — the trace of what the original could not be allowed to say. (Sibling instances in the archive: canonized codas, the "long ending" phenomenon, harmonizations, softened translations.)
