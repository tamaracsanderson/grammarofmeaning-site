## How to read this

This is a field guide, not a textbook. A textbook wants to be read in order and believed; a field guide
wants to be opened at any section, argued with, and used. Its sections are not chapters someone chose — they
are the **four categories the reading method applies to every text it reads**: **Sitz** (the world it came
from), **Paradigm** (the story-shapes it thinks with), **Frame** (how it knows what it claims), and
**Lineage** (what it stands on). Read one, or read them in order; each opens onto the others.

Two things to know before you start. First, the reading is produced with an instrument — a method, run with
AI, that reads each text one action at a time (in the empty-tomb scene, rolling the stone, entering, and
speaking are each recorded separately) and notes both what each action says and what it leaves unsaid — not
written from memory or belief. Every factual claim traces to a named scholar or a public source; every claim
that is the instrument's own reading is marked as that; where the scholarship is divided, this page shows the
sides. The full account of the method and its sources is the last section, **"Where did this come from."**
Second, the worked example throughout is the gospels' **resurrection** accounts (Matthew 28, Mark 16, Luke
24, John 20) — the first passages this reading room (the interactive companion these pages belong to) has
coded.

## Frontispiece — what these four texts are

Four writers, working in the last third of the first century, each set down an account of the life,
teaching, death, and reported resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth. Christians call these accounts the Gospels
— Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John — and treat them as the central record of who Jesus was and what his life
meant. All four were written roughly fifty years after Jesus died, and all came after the letters of the
apostle Paul (a Jewish follower of Jesus who never met him in life, whose letters from the 50s CE are the
earliest surviving Christian writings, a generation older than any gospel). Early tradition gave each an
emblem still used in art — Matthew a winged man, Mark a lion, Luke an ox, John an eagle — the four "living
creatures" of Ezekiel's and Revelation's visions, matched to the four gospels by early Christian writers
such as Irenaeus and Jerome.

The four rhyme, then diverge. Their resurrection accounts share a sequence of beats — women come to the tomb
at first light, find the stone already moved, meet a shining figure who says Jesus has risen, and leave to
carry the news — and they differ within it: how many women come, whether one figure waits at the tomb or
two, whether the risen Jesus is promised in Galilee or seen in Jerusalem. This reading room examines those
differences closely. But first it situates the four texts, through the four lenses below.

## Chapter 1 · Sitz — the world they came out of
*The situation that produced a text: when it was written, where, and what the world was doing to the people
who wrote it.*

**In this chapter:** The shared world · The timeline, with places · When each was written · Where each was
written, and for whom · Why they wrote it down.

The four gospels share one situation and one central response to it. They were written after a war destroyed
the Temple, the center of Jewish worship — and each answers that catastrophe the same way: by arguing that
the community gathered around the risen Jesus, not the Temple, is now where God is present. The rest of this
chapter fills that in — the shared world, the timeline, when and where each was written, and why they were
written at all.

### The shared world — *the four as a whole*

Jesus lived and died in the Roman province of Judea (on the eastern shore of the Mediterranean, in what is
now Israel and the Palestinian territories), in and around Galilee and Jerusalem, about the year 30. The
province was under Roman rule. The four gospels were written decades later, and in the years between fell
the event that shaped all four: a war that destroyed the center of Jewish worship.

Between 66 and 73/74 CE the population of Judea rose in revolt against Rome. Rome answered with legions,
reconquered the province, and in August of the year 70 the Roman general Titus captured Jerusalem and
destroyed the Second Temple — the second on that site, rebuilt around 515 BCE after Babylon had destroyed
Solomon's first temple and carried the Judeans into exile (the Babylonian exile of the sixth century BCE).
That Temple was the single place where sacrifice to the God of Israel was offered, and the pilgrimage center
Jews traveled to from across the empire for festivals. Its destruction ended the Temple's sacrificial
worship (animal and grain offerings made by a priesthood at that one authorized altar), which had organized
Jewish religious life for centuries.

When the Temple was gone, every surviving Jewish group faced the same practical question: who now decides
what God requires, and where do people go to meet God, once the one central place is rubble? On a common
scholarly reading, the four gospels are in part one answer to that question: each argues that the community
gathered around the risen Jesus, not the Temple, is now where God is present. That is what it means to say
each moves the center of religious life off the destroyed Temple and onto the risen Christ — a presence the
community gathers around not at an altar but in shared meals, baptism, prayer, and the reading aloud of texts
like these.

Two facts about timing carry most of the weight. Jesus lived before the Temple fell; every gospel was
written after, so the catastrophe stands behind the writers and shapes how they tell even the resurrection.
And the gospels were written not in the wrecked homeland but in the cities of the diaspora, the Jewish (and
now Christian) communities living outside the Judean homeland, scattered and largely Greek-speaking, that
centuries of migration had spread across the Roman Mediterranean.

### The timeline, with places — *the four as a whole*

- **~30–33 CE** — Jesus of Nazareth: his teaching and his death, in Galilee and Jerusalem.
- **~50s CE** — the letters of Paul, the earliest surviving Christian writings.
- **~64–67 CE** — the emperor Nero blames Christians for a great fire at Rome and has them executed; the
  traditional deaths of the apostles Peter and Paul, the loss of the founding eyewitnesses that later
  helped prompt the written gospels.
- **66–73/74 CE** — the First Jewish–Roman War: the Judean revolt and its defeat.
- **August 70 CE** — Roman forces destroy Jerusalem and the Second Temple.
- **~70 CE** — the Gospel of Mark, the earliest.
- **~80s CE** — the Gospel of Matthew.
- **~85 CE** — the Gospel of Luke (with its second volume, the Acts of the Apostles).
- **~90–110 CE** — the Gospel of John, the latest.

The one thing to carry: the gospels stand about forty to seventy years after Jesus, in the order Mark,
Matthew, Luke, John, all written after the Temple fell.

### When each was written — *the four as a whole*

Scholars date the four from two firm anchors. The first is that **Mark came first**: nearly all scholars
hold that Matthew and Luke each worked with a copy of Mark in front of them, expanding and rearranging it.
The second is the year 70, because the gospels appear to know Jerusalem has already fallen. In Matthew, a
king in a parable sends troops who "destroyed those murderers, and burned their city," a line most scholars
read as written after 70, with the destruction already in view (Davies and Allison, in the standard
critical commentary, read it this way, as a likely rather than a certain reading). A text that describes the
fall of Jerusalem in specific detail was most likely written after it happened.

One date is genuinely disputed. Most scholars place Luke in the 80s or 90s; a serious minority, led by
Richard Pervo and Joseph Tyson, argues for the early second century (about 100 to 120), partly because Luke
seems to know the writings of the Jewish historian Josephus, whose major work appeared in the 90s. This page
follows the majority date and marks the later view as a real scholarly position.

### Where each was written, and for whom — *each gospel on its own*

No gospel names its own place of writing. Scholars reconstruct each one's likely home from its language, the
customs it pauses to explain, the tensions it reveals, and the earliest outside writers who quote it. What
follows is probability, not an address.

- **Mark** — by the oldest tradition, Rome, for a largely Gentile (non-Jewish) audience: it carries many
  Latin loanwords and stops to translate Aramaic and explain Jewish customs. A serious modern alternative
  places it in Syria. The careful statement is Rome by tradition, with Syria a real possibility.
- **Matthew** — most likely Syria, and Antioch (today Antakya, in southern Turkey) is the usual guess. Its
  community is Jewish and Christian at once, arguing with the emerging rabbinic movement (the teachers who,
  after the Temple fell, were shaping what became mainstream rabbinic Judaism) over who rightly interprets
  the Torah; it speaks of "their synagogues." Ignatius, a bishop of Antioch writing around 110, is among the
  earliest to quote it.
- **Luke** — the hardest to place: an unknown Greek-speaking city in the eastern Mediterranean. Luke writes
  the most polished Greek of the four and carries the fewest local markers. It addresses a "Theophilus"
  (either a patron or, since the name means "lover of God," a stand-in for the ideal reader) and pitches the
  story to a largely Gentile audience.
- **John** — by tradition Ephesus (on the Aegean coast of western Turkey); some argue for Syria. It reads
  like the voice of a community of Jewish followers of Jesus pushed out of their local synagogue, a rupture
  that colors its sharp language of us and them, light and darkness.

One caution: scholars once spoke confidently of each gospel's own local "community." Richard Bauckham argued
the gospels were written for wide circulation, not one town. The community model is still the working
majority view; his challenge is the reason to hold it loosely.

### Why they wrote it down — *the four as a whole*

For a generation the movement lived on spoken testimony from people who had known Jesus, and many expected
the world to end within their lifetimes. Several pressures then converged and made written accounts
necessary. The eyewitnesses were dying: Peter and Paul were gone. Many early Christians had expected Jesus
to return within their own lifetimes to judge and remake the world; when that end of the age did not arrive,
the movement had to prepare for a longer future. The communities were scattered and Greek-speaking, and
needed durable accounts to instruct new members, to worship, and to settle disputes at a distance. And the
destruction of the Temple in 70 pressed them to set down the full story of Jesus' life and death, not only
the short summaries of belief they had used until then. Those written accounts became the shared story that
scattered communities read aloud, taught, and argued their beliefs and practices from — the common ground on
which the church's later settled forms (a fixed order of worship, creeds, an agreed list of scriptures, an
ordained clergy) would eventually be worked out, none of which existed yet.

## Chapter 2 · Paradigm — the story-shapes they think with
*The familiar cultural scripts a text reaches for — patterns its first readers already recognized.*

**In this chapter:** The script all four share · Four inflections.

### The script all four share — *the four braided*

Reading the four resurrection accounts, the instrument finds one script all four reach for: the
**vindication of the wrongly condemned righteous one**. This was a live expectation in the Judaism of the
time, visible in Jewish writings from the centuries just before Jesus (the Wisdom of Solomon, a Greek Jewish
wisdom book, and the book of Daniel, a Jewish apocalypse): that God will publicly reverse an unjust verdict
passed on a faithful person. In all four gospels, the empty tomb is that reversal. Each gospel then shapes
the shared script its own way.

### Four inflections — *each gospel on its own*

- **Matthew — public overturning.** The armed guards at the tomb, agents of the power that executed Jesus,
  are struck down "like dead men," while the executed man is raised and low-status women carry the news
  first. Matthew tells it in the register of a **theophany** (a scene in which God visibly breaks into the
  world), with an earthquake and an angel whose face is "like lightning," the kind of scene the Hebrew Bible
  reserves for God's own appearing, as at Mount Sinai, where the book of Exodus has Moses meet God.
- **Mark — proclamation withheld.** Mark ends not in triumph but in fear. In its earliest manuscripts the
  gospel stops with the women fleeing the tomb and saying "nothing to anyone, for they were afraid" (later
  scribes, uneasy with so abrupt a close, added the longer endings found in most Bibles). The proclamation is
  left undelivered, handed to the reader.
- **Luke — vindication through memory.** The empty tomb means nothing until the women recall Jesus' own
  earlier words; and the gospel pointedly credits their testimony where their culture, which discounted
  women as legal witnesses, would have dismissed it.
- **John — recognition, and the divine Word.** John's resurrection is a scene of seeing and believing, built
  around the moment a disciple looks at the folded graveclothes and grasps what they mean. John also goes
  further than the others: most explicitly of the four, it identifies Jesus with a pre-existent divine Word
  (the others carry at most hints of the idea). Where they open with his birth or ministry, John opens
  before creation — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."
  Scholars call the underlying question the debate over "high christology": did the earliest Christians
  already hold Jesus to be divine, with John making it explicit, or did that belief grow across the decades
  and reach its fullest form only here?

## Chapter 3 · Frame — how each gospel knows what it claims
*The grounds a text rests its claims on — the kind of authority it leans on when it asks you to believe.*

**In this chapter:** Two grounds, revelation and testimony · John's added ground.

### Two grounds: revelation and testimony — *the four braided*

The four differ not only in what they report but in how they claim to know it. Two grounds recur —
**revelation** (the news announced by a messenger) and **testimony** (the news reported by a witness who was
there) — and every gospel uses both; what differs is which one *leads*. **Matthew and Mark lead with
revelation**: a messenger announces the news before anyone weighs evidence. **Luke and John lead with
testimony**: the scene is built as a chain of who saw what, in what order. Luke is the hybrid case: it leads
with eyewitness report, but it keeps the old announced formula, "He is not here, for he has risen," the same
words Matthew and Mark use. Only **John** drops that formula entirely — no messenger says it — and its empty
tomb is shown and inspected rather than proclaimed.

### John's added ground: physical evidence — *John on its own*

John rests weight on something the other three do not: the physical remains. It lingers on the burial cloths
left in the empty tomb, and on the cloth that had covered the head, "rolled up in a place by itself." In
John the empty tomb is not only announced and witnessed but inspected. The gospel reasons from the evidence
to the best explanation of it: grave-robbers do not unwrap a body and fold the headcloth aside, so, the
gospel implies, the orderly tomb points away from theft. The others mostly proclaim the empty tomb; John
examines it most closely.

## Chapter 4 · Lineage — what they stand on
*The earlier sources a text is built on — the scripture it quotes, the path by which it reaches us — and
what shifts when it borrows.*

**In this chapter:** What the gospels build on · The scripture the resurrection stands on · How the text
comes down to us.

### What the gospels build on — *the four as a whole*

No gospel starts from nothing; each stands on earlier material. Three sources matter most.

- **The Hebrew scriptures** — the Jewish scriptures (what Christians came to call the Old Testament),
  usually quoted in their Greek translation, the **Septuagint** (the Greek version of the Hebrew scriptures,
  made in the third and second centuries BCE, that most Greek-speaking Jews and early Christians actually
  read). The gospels read Jesus' life as the fulfillment of these texts, and borrow their scenes and
  language constantly.
- **Oral testimony and Paul's letters** — the spoken accounts of people who had known Jesus, and Paul's
  letters from the 50s, both circulating before any gospel was written.
- **Mark** — for Matthew and Luke, the most direct source of all. Each built on Mark's text, which is why
  the first three gospels (called the **Synoptics**, from a Greek word for "seen together," because they can
  be set in parallel columns) share so much wording and sequence. John draws instead on its own distinct
  stream of tradition.

### The scripture the resurrection stands on — *the four braided*

The gospels are Jewish texts arguing from Jewish scripture, and the instrument can show exactly where. Run on
the four resurrection chapters, its lineage detector (the part of the instrument that traces a passage's
scriptural sources) found 24 places — nine in Matthew, five in Mark, seven in Luke, three in John — where
the accounts stand on the Hebrew scriptures. The firmest name the load-bearing borrowings:

| Scripture | What it grounds | In |
|---|---|---|
| **Psalm 16:10** — "you will not let your holy one see corruption" | the warrant that death could not hold him | John (the only one a gospel quotes explicitly as scripture), echoed across the others |
| **Hosea 6:2** — "on the third day he will raise us up" | the resurrection's **third-day** timetable | Luke, Matthew |
| **Daniel 7 & 10** — the Son of Man; the angel "like lightning," clothing white | the shining messenger at the tomb | Matthew (its angel is built from Daniel's angel-visions), Luke |
| **Isaiah 53** — the suffering servant, "cut off" for others | the executed-then-vindicated righteous one | Luke, Matthew |
| **Zechariah 13:7** — "strike the shepherd, and the sheep scatter" | the scattering of the disciples | Mark |
| **Exodus 25:18–22** — the two winged cherubim flanking the mercy-seat (the golden lid of the ark of the covenant, Israel's holiest object) | John's **two angels** at the head and foot of the empty place — the tomb as a new holy of holies | John |

What the instrument adds is a measure of the **shift** — how far the borrowed words are bent from their
original sense to a new claim. Hosea's "third day" was about the whole people's restoration; the gospels
bend it to one man's rising. Psalm 16 was about being spared death; they read it as raised *through* death.
The borrowed words are made to mean something their source did not quite say, and that bending is where the
gospel makes its own claim.

Some echoes the instrument marks as open questions, not findings — the evidence is suggestive but not firm,
so it offers them for you to weigh. Is John's "first day of the week … while it was still dark" standing on
**Genesis 1**'s first day and its light-from-darkness, framing the resurrection as a new creation? Does
Matthew's earthquake-and-rising echo **Ezekiel 37**'s valley of dry bones coming to life?

### How the text comes down to us — *the four as a whole*

All four gospels are **anonymous**: no author is named inside any of them. The familiar titles ("according
to Matthew") were attached later, in the late first or early second century, to tie each book to the
authority of an apostle; Bart Ehrman is the scholar most associated with stating this plainly. The earliest
outside comment on how the gospels were made comes from Papias, a bishop writing around 110 to 125 (quoted
much later by Eusebius, the fourth-century church historian), who reports that Mark wrote down the
recollections of the apostle Peter. On the details, scholars judge that Mark was an unknown Greek-speaking Christian; that Matthew was a
Jewish-Christian scribe rather than the apostle of that name, since his gospel reproduces most of Mark's text
and an eyewitness would be expected to write from his own memory; that Luke was a well-read Greek writer who
also wrote Acts; and that John took shape in stages around the Beloved Disciple (an unnamed disciple who
appears only in John, presented as the eyewitness behind that gospel). The oldest surviving physical scrap of
any gospel is a fragment of John, a papyrus from the first half of the second century.

## Where did this come from

*The process, the coded data, and the sources behind everything above.*

### How this reading is made

Behind this page is an **instrument** — a reading method, run with AI, that reads a text one action at a
time. For each action it records who does what to whom, and it also records what the action leaves unanswered
(in the empty-tomb scene, for example, the stone "was rolled away," and the text never says by whom). It then
situates the text through the four lenses this page is built on — Sitz, Paradigm, Frame, Lineage — the same
four it applies to any text the room takes up.

Two kinds of material meet here, and the page keeps them distinct. The **historical facts** (the war, the
dates, the places, the authorship) come from mainstream biblical scholarship, gathered and cross-checked
across multiple independent AI-assisted literature passes and reported here only where those passes agreed;
the named scholars below are the ones that agreement rested on. The **readings** (which script each gospel
thinks with, how each claims to know, what scripture each stands on) are the instrument's own coded output,
and they are marked as such. Where scholarship is divided, the page shows the sides; where the instrument's
own confidence is low, it says so and offers the point as a question. The method is authored and AI-assisted,
and it is offered as a contribution in its own right, not a workaround for lacking a human scholar. It is
built to work against two biases that quietly shape any single reading: the pull toward the best-documented
traditions and best-known scholars — the loudest voices in the record — and the training skew of any one AI
model. Rather than claim to escape those biases by being neutral, it works against them by being checkable:
every factual claim is run past several independent models and kept only where they agree; each is tied to a
named source; and the works the reading leans on that the library cannot yet show are named as gaps rather
than hidden. The method can still introduce bias of its own — the safeguard is that its choices are
auditable, not that they are unbiased.

### The coded data behind each lens

- **Sitz** — dating spine **Mark ~70 · Matthew ~80 · Luke ~85 · John ~95** (coder-supplied year anchors,
  consistent with mainstream scholarship, not a substitute for its reasoning). Tradition-phase, all four:
  **Formation** — the early stage at which a tradition is still building the structures (settled worship,
  creeds, canon, clergy) it will later treat as given.
- **Paradigm** — dominant script, all four, *endorsed*: the **vindication of the righteous sufferer**.
  Per-gospel scripts: Matthew, theophany + honor-reversal · Mark, the withheld/ironized proclamation · Luke,
  memory + the vindicated witnesses · John, recognition + the divine Word.
- **Frame** — the ground each gospel leads with: **Matthew · Mark = revelation** (announced); **Luke · John
  = testimony** (witnessed). John alone also rests weight on the **physical** evidence (the grave-cloths).
- **Lineage** — **24 scripture-borrowings** found on the four resurrection chapters (Matthew 9 · Mark 5 ·
  Luke 7 · John 3); the firm ones are the table above, the suggestive-but-unproven ones are flagged as open
  questions. Source: the S158 lineage detector on MATT-28 / MARK-16 / LUKE-24 / JOHN-20.

### What is firm, and what is still debated

Most of this page is broad scholarly consensus: Mark's priority, the year 70 as the hinge, the anonymity of
all four gospels, the general dates, and the reading of the war as the shaping crisis. A few points remain
open, and the page marks them: the exact date of Luke (majority 80s–90s, a serious minority into the early
second century); the precise city each gospel was written in (best guesses, not settled facts); and whether
each gospel was written for one local congregation or, as Richard Bauckham argued, for wide circulation. One
older view held that around the year 90 a Jewish synagogue benediction (the Birkat ha-Minim, a curse on
"heretics") formally expelled Christians in a single stroke — the kind of expulsion John's community,
"pushed out of the synagogue," seems to reflect. Reuven Kimelman and Daniel Boyarin have argued,
persuasively, that the separation of Judaism and Christianity was instead gradual and piecemeal — a "parting
of the ways" that took generations, not one decree.
